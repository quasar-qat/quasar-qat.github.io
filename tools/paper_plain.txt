# QUASAR: Lowering the Loss Floor of Quantization-Aware Training with Loss-Aware Reconstruction

## Abstract
As large language model inference shifts toward lower precision, post-training quantization (PTQ) becomes increasingly brittle, making quantization-aware training (QAT) essential for preserving model quality. However, QAT has a structural mismatch: gradient updates are applied to latent full-precision weights, while the loss and gradients are computed on lossy reconstructions of those weights. This mismatch can lead to suboptimal training trajectories and a higher loss floor. Second-order PTQ methods address a similar problem by minimizing loss-aware reconstruction error, but applying such expensive reconstruction repeatedly during QAT as the weights evolve is impractical. We introduce QUASAR, a QAT method that brings lightweight, loss-aware reconstruction into the training loop. At each training step, QUASAR reconstructs the latent weights by searching over a small set of clipping ranges and fitting dequantization parameters through saliency-weighted least squares. We use an exponential moving average of squared gradients as the per-parameter saliency signal. Our theoretical analysis shows that optimizing QUASAR's reconstruction objective tightens both the convergence and final-loss bounds of QAT. We evaluate QUASAR across four model families and across INT4, INT3, INT2, and NVFP4 quantization formats. QUASAR consistently achieves lower training and evaluation loss than competitive QAT methods and outperforms QAT and PTQ baselines on downstream benchmarks. At INT2, QUASAR improves average accuracy over the best QAT baseline by 13.3 points with quantization-aware distillation and by 10.9 points with QAT on mathematical reasoning data. Notably, after distillation on only ~600M tokens, QUASAR's INT4 Gemma-4 E4B checkpoint outperforms the corresponding QAT checkpoint released by Google, with 66% lower KL divergence and 1.8 points higher average accuracy.

## 1 Introduction
As large language models (LLMs) grow in size and adoption, inference is overtaking training as the dominant recurring cost of deployment. To reduce this cost, inference is rapidly moving toward low-precision formats such as FP4 and INT4, which shrink the model's memory footprint, reduce decoding latency, and enable higher concurrency and throughput. However, since models are typically trained in high precision but served in low precision, preserving model quality as precision becomes increasingly aggressive remains a critical challenge. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has been the standard way to convert a trained model to low precision, but it is becoming increasingly brittle on newer models. Reasoning and agentic models often operate over long contexts, where quantization errors can accumulate and degrade performance. PTQ models may also exhibit behavioral shifts and biases relative to their full-precision counterparts. Producing a native low-precision model with stronger quality guarantees therefore requires training under the same precision used for serving.
Figure 1: The structural mismatch of gradients in standard QAT versus QUASAR. Under the STE, both methods evaluate the gradient at the reconstructed weights r and apply it to the latent weights w. QUASAR minimizes the loss-aware reconstruction error, making the gradient at r a more faithful proxy for the gradient at w.
Quantization-aware training (QAT) addresses this need by inserting quantization into the training loop, allowing the model to adapt to quantization noise as it learns. QAT, however, introduces a structural mismatch into optimization (Figure 1): the forward pass and loss computation use quantized–dequantized reconstruction weights r, while the gradient updates are applied to the latent full-precision weights w. Because quantization has zero gradient almost everywhere, QAT typically uses the straight-through estimator (STE), approximating ∂ℒ/∂w with ∂ℒ/∂r. This surrogate is generally not the optimal descent direction for the latent weights, leading training along a suboptimal trajectory. The consequence is a loss-floor gap: given the same model and training data, QAT converges to a higher final training and evaluation loss than full-precision training. Prior QAT methods narrow this gap by softening the rounding operation, learning quantization parameters jointly with the weights, or improving initialization. We instead draw on a complementary insight from second-order PTQ methods: the excess training loss caused by QAT's structural mismatch can be reduced by optimizing reconstruction.
A second-order expansion of the loss around the full-precision weights w, assuming that the first-order term is negligible near an optimum, gives ℒ(r)−ℒ(w) ≈ 1/2 S, S=(r−w)ᵀH(r−w), where H(w) is the Hessian evaluated at w. We refer to S as the loss-aware reconstruction error. Many modern PTQ methods minimize this quantity, or an approximation to it, to construct a low-precision model whose loss remains close to that of a frozen full-precision model. Applying the same principle to QAT would keep the low-precision loss ℒ(r) closer to the full-precision loss ℒ(w) as the latent weights evolve, thereby mitigating the effect of the structural mismatch. The challenge, however, is computational cost. In PTQ, the full-precision model is frozen, so its reconstruction needs to be optimized only once, typically requiring minutes to hours for a billion-parameter LLM. In QAT, however, the latent weights change at every step, requiring the reconstruction to be continually re-optimized. Running a conventional second-order optimization procedure on every forward pass would therefore be prohibitively expensive.
We introduce QUASAR (Quantization-aware training with loss-aware reconstruction), a QAT method that continually minimizes loss-aware reconstruction error to improve the training trajectory and lower the loss floor. QUASAR decomposes reconstruction into two stages: quantization, which maps full-precision weights to discrete codes while treating the clipping range as a free parameter, and dequantization, which maps those codes back to reconstructed weights using a learned scale and offset. To make the loss-aware objective tractable during training, QUASAR approximates the Hessian using an exponential moving average of squared gradients, yielding per-parameter saliency scores that estimate each weight's effect on the loss. It then searches over a small set of candidate clipping ranges, each defining a different assignment of discrete codes; for each assignment, it solves for the dequantization parameters in closed form by saliency-weighted least squares and selects the candidate with the smallest loss-aware reconstruction error. Our theoretical analysis shows that reducing this error tightens the QAT convergence bound and final-loss bound (Section 4).
Our contributions are threefold. (i) We identify loss-aware reconstruction error, previously optimized for frozen models in second-order PTQ, as a principled objective for QAT. We introduce QUASAR to minimize this objective at every training step using an exponential moving average of squared gradients, closed-form saliency-weighted least squares, and a small clipping-range search. (ii) Under empirically validated assumptions, we prove that reducing this error tightens bounds on both QAT convergence and the loss of the final quantized model. (iii) Across four model families and four quantization formats, QUASAR consistently reduces loss and improves downstream performance over competitive QAT and PTQ baselines. At INT2, it improves average accuracy by up to 13.3 points over the strongest QAT baseline; at INT4, its Gemma-4 E4B checkpoint achieves 66% lower held-out KL and 1.8 points higher average accuracy than Google's public QAT checkpoint.

## 2 Related Work
QUASAR sits between two lines of work: PTQ and QAT. PTQ treats quantization as a one-shot algorithm: given a trained model and a small calibration set, select low-precision values that damage the loss least, typically by minimizing a second-order expansion of the loss or learning the rounding against a reconstruction objective. All rest on the premise that the weights are frozen, so the reconstruction happens only once. A second PTQ line spreads outliers with random orthogonal transforms and replaces the quantization grid with lattice, trellis, or additive codebooks; these are the strongest PTQ methods at two and three bits, but their inference-time transforms and decodes hurt throughput, while hardware moves toward simpler formats such as MXFP4 and NVFP4.
Below four bits, the PTQ methods that do not add inference overhead degrade sharply. QAT reaches higher quality at these bit widths by learning step sizes, supervising with the full-precision model, or freezing the quantized weights and training low-rank adapters; none of these optimize the map from latent weights to reconstructed weights, which is set by heuristics. QUASAR directly optimizes that map: it brings the reconstruction PTQ solves once into the training loop as the weights adapt, while still deploying exactly as an RTN-quantized model. Other methods are complementary and compose with QUASAR: EfficientQAT's second stage learns only the step sizes, the same quantizer parameter as LSQ, and its block-wise first stage is a complementary initialization; ParetoQ splits a fixed training budget between full-precision training and QAT.

## 3 Methodology
We first describe weight reconstruction in standard QAT and explain how reconstruction error affects the training loss. We then introduce QUASAR, which makes this reconstruction loss-aware through an efficient curvature approximation, scale search over clipping ranges, and optimal dequantization.

### 3.1 Preliminary: Reconstruction in QAT
QAT maintains and updates full-precision latent weights w, but computes the forward pass and loss using reconstructed weights r. At each step, QAT maps w to low-bit codes q and then dequantizes those codes to obtain r: w ↦ q ↦ r. We refer to this process as weight reconstruction. It exposes the model to inference-time quantization error during training while allowing the latent weights to adapt. Because the code assignment involves rounding and has no useful derivative, QAT uses the straight-through estimator (STE): ∂ℒ/∂w ≈ ∂ℒ/∂r. The gradient evaluated at r is therefore applied to w (Figure 1).
For asymmetric integer quantization, each weight matrix is partitioned into groups of g contiguous weights, commonly with g=32 or 128. A group w∈ℝ^g is represented by b-bit integer codes q_i∈{0,…,2^b−1}. All weights in the group share a scale s and an offset z, which determine the quantization grid. Quantization assigns codes and dequantization reconstructs weights as q_i = clamp_{[0,2^b−1]}(⌊ (w_i−z)/s ⌉), r_i=sq_i+z. Reconstruction has two stages: code assignment, w ↦ q, and dequantization, q ↦ r. Standard QAT uses the minimum and maximum weights in each group for both stages, setting z=min_i w_i and s=(max_i w_i−min_i w_i)/(2^b−1). This uniform grid represents the full range of weight values without clipping and preserves the extreme values in the group, but it may not be optimal for reducing the training loss.
Reconstruction quality directly affects the training loss of QAT. At step t, consider the difference between the loss at the reconstructed weights and the loss at the latent weights, ℒ(r_t)−ℒ(w_t). When the first-order term is negligible, as near a stationary region, a second-order expansion around w_t, with H_t=∇²ℒ(w_t), gives ℒ(r_t)−ℒ(w_t) ≈ 1/2 S_t, where S_t=(r_t−w_t)ᵀH_t(r_t−w_t). We call S_t the loss-aware reconstruction error. It measures the local change in loss caused by reconstructing the latent weights. Because both the forward pass and the STE gradient are evaluated at r_t, this error can affect every optimization step. Second-order PTQ methods minimize this objective, or a related approximation, once for a frozen model, but the process can take hours for a large model. Repeating this optimization at every QAT training step is impractical because the latent weights change after every update. QUASAR instead minimizes a close approximation to S_t at every training step using a lightweight procedure that combines scale search with a closed-form fit for dequantization.

### 3.2 A Tractable Loss-Aware Objective
QUASAR aims to minimize the loss-aware reconstruction error S_t in Equation (2). The exact Hessian H in S_t is intractable to compute, so an approximation is required. One could consider computing the Hessian Vector Product (HVP), but it is also too expensive since it requires computing an HVP for every candidate reconstruction for every training step. We therefore use the diagonal Fisher information matrix as a proxy for the Hessian. Following Li et al. (2025a), we approximate it with an exponential moving average (EMA) of squared gradients. Adam and AdamW already maintain this quantity as the second-moment estimate v_t, allowing QUASAR to reuse it without additional memory overhead. The EMA also smooths noise from individual training steps.
Let g_i=∂ℒ/∂w_i be the gradient with respect to the latent weight w_i, and define h_i=EMA(g_i²). We use h_i as the ith diagonal entry of the Hessian approximation, giving the objective S_t ≈ Ŝ_t = Σ_i h_i (r_i−w_i)². We call h_i the saliency of w_i. Saliency varies substantially within a weight group (Appendix A, Figure 8). Because all g weights in a group share dequantization parameters, reducing the reconstruction error of one weight can increase that of another. The weights h_i govern this tradeoff: QUASAR prioritizes accurate reconstruction of high-saliency weights while tolerating more error on low-saliency weights (Appendix A, Figure 5).

### 3.3 Optimizing Both Stages of Reconstruction
To minimize the objective in Equation (3), QUASAR optimizes code assignment w ↦ q and dequantization q ↦ r separately. Standard QAT uses the same scale and offset to assign codes and reconstruct weights. In contrast, QUASAR searches over candidate clipping scales to find code assignments and, for each assignment, computes the optimal dequantization scale and offset in closed form. It then selects the candidate with the smallest loss-aware reconstruction error. Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure.
A weighted least-squares fit gives the optimal dequantization. For a fixed code assignment q, QUASAR solves for (s,z) in r_i=sq_i+z to minimize Equation (3). This is a weighted linear regression and therefore has a closed-form solution (Equation (6), Appendix A.1).
Scale search finds good code assignments. The closed-form fit is optimal for a fixed q, but the code assignment itself may be far from optimal. QUASAR therefore searches over scale factors that shrink the clipping range. Each range creates a different code assignment. For a group with midpoint c and half-range ρ, a factor f∈(0,1] defines the assignment range I_f, where c= (min_i w_i+max_i w_i)/2, ρ= (max_i w_i−min_i w_i)/2, I_f=[c−fρ, c+fρ]. The case f=1 is the full min–max range used by standard QAT. A factor f<1 narrows the range and clips weights outside it, so the quantization levels cover a smaller interval with finer spacing. In our integer experiments, we search ℱ={0.30,0.35,…,1.00}.
For each candidate f∈ℱ, QUASAR first uses the corresponding clipping range to assign codes q_f. It then fits the optimal dequantization parameters (s_f,z_f) for q_f using Equation (6) and computes the resulting error Ŝ_t(f). After evaluating all candidates, QUASAR uses the reconstruction associated with f⋆=argmin_{f∈ℱ} Ŝ_t(f). This search is performed as part of each QAT forward pass. Its empirical behavior is shown in Figures 6 and 7 in Appendix A.
QUASAR changes only the weight reconstruction used in the forward pass. It leaves activation quantization and backpropagation unchanged: gradients still pass through the weight quantizer with the standard STE, and neither scale search nor dequantization fitting requires a separate backward rule. The same two-stage procedure applies to asymmetric and symmetric integer formats as well as floating-point formats such as NVFP4 and MXFP4 (the corresponding algorithms are presented in Appendix A.1). We evaluate QUASAR for NVFP4 in Section 5.2.

## 4 Theory: Reconstruction Error Bounds QAT Final Loss
In this section, we show that QUASAR lowers the excess loss of the final quantized model, ℒ(r_T)−ℒ⋆. This gap is bounded by QUASAR's loss-aware reconstruction error, so minimizing that error is a natural target. Under the identity STE, the gradient evaluated at the reconstruction r_t is applied to the latent weights w_t, creating the gradient mismatch e_t:=∇ℒ(r_t)−∇ℒ(w_t). Prior work has studied when STE gradients remain useful descent directions, how curvature shapes QAT plateaus, and how a systematic gradient bias slows SGD. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has studied the relationship between the reconstruction error of the quantizer to the convergence of QAT or to the loss of the final quantized model. Let ℒ:ℝ^d → ℝ be the training objective with ℒ⋆:=inf_x ℒ(x), and T ≥ 1 be the number of training steps. At step t, w_t, r_t∈ℝ^d are the latent and reconstructed weights, h_t∈ℝ_+^d the saliencies, and Ŝ_t:=Σ_i h_{t,i}(r_{t,i}−w_{t,i})² the objective of Section 3 at step t. 𝔼_t[·] denotes expectation conditional on the training history ℋ_t, which determines h_t and r_t.
Assumptions. We make four assumptions. (1) The objective ℒ is well behaved: twice continuously differentiable, L-smooth, and bounded below. (2) The STE gradient is the true gradient at the reconstruction plus zero-mean noise: G_t=∇ℒ(r_t)+ξ_t with 𝔼_t[ξ_t]=0 and 𝔼_t‖ξ_t‖² ≤ M‖∇ℒ(r_t)‖²+σ² for constants M,σ² ≥ 0, the biased-SGD noise condition; the update is w_{t+1}=w_t−ηG_t. (3) The reconstruction error controls the gradient mismatch: for some finite constant C and every t∈{0,…,T}, ‖∇ℒ(r_t)−∇ℒ(w_t)‖² ≤ C Ŝ_t. Appendix B.1 gives a sufficient curvature condition with C=L²/h_min under a saliency floor h_min >0, Figure 14 (Appendix C) confirms the inequality empirically at all tested bit widths, and Appendix B.2 discusses the assumptions further. Appendix A.2 compares the H- and H²-motivated saliency weightings. (4) The objective satisfies the global Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality ‖∇ℒ(x)‖² ≥ 2μ(ℒ(x)−ℒ⋆) for all x∈ℝ^d and some μ>0; it is needed only for the final-model bound.
Theorem 1 (Reconstruction error controls QAT convergence and final loss). Under Assumptions 1–3, let 0<η< 1/(L(1+M)). The following statements hold.
(i) Convergence. 1/T Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)𝔼[‖∇ℒ(w_t)‖² +(1−Lη(1+M))‖∇ℒ(r_t)‖²] ≤ (2(ℒ(w_0)−ℒ⋆))/ηT +Lησ² + C/T Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)𝔼Ŝ_t.
(ii) Final quantized model. If Assumption 4 also holds and η< 1/μ, then, for a random training iterate τ (Appendix B.1), 𝔼[ℒ(r_T)−ℒ⋆] ≤ 2L/μ (1−ημ)^T (ℒ(w_0)−ℒ⋆) + LC/μ² 𝔼Ŝ_τ + L²ησ²/μ² + C/μ 𝔼Ŝ_T.
Proofs are presented in Appendix B. Theorem 1 shows that the QAT convergence bound contains the usual stochastic-optimization terms (initialization, minibatch noise) plus a quantization term given by the loss-aware reconstruction error. QUASAR directly minimizes this term at every step, hence tightening the bound on the quantized model's final loss. Appendix C supports the theorem's assumptions (Figures 14 and 16), evaluates the bound (Figure 17), shows that reconstruction error tracks final loss (Figures 10, 12, and 13), and repeats the comparison under SGD (Figure 18).

## 5 Experiments
In this section, we evaluate QUASAR against competitive QAT and PTQ baselines. We assess its ability to preserve quantized model quality using Quantization-aware Distillation (QAD), to adapt quantized models to learn new tasks using QAT, and to generalize to different quantization formats including INT4/INT3/INT2 and NVFP4. Moreover, we compare QUASAR QAT'ed checkpoints against the QAT checkpoints released by Google under the same format.
Setup and baselines. For healing, we apply QAD to Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at INT2, INT3, and INT4 using Open-PerfectBlend data and logits from the full-precision teachers. For adaptation, we train all weights of Qwen3-4B-Base on OpenMathReasoning using cross-entropy. We also apply QAD in NVFP4 to Qwen3-8B, Qwen3.5-9B, and Muse-Glimmer-30B, and evaluate QUASAR against Google's QAT checkpoint, Gemma 4, and other independent checkpoints. Baselines in each experiment use the same data, objective, training budget, and tuned learning rate, with matched inference formats. We compare against Standard QAT (Section 3.1), LSQ, Denoising QAT, BitDistiller, RTN, GPTQ, and AWQ; for GGUF, we also include imatrix PTQ, which uses calibration activations to estimate weight importance. Full training protocols and hyperparameters are in Appendix D.
Evaluation. We evaluate quantized checkpoints and report fidelity to the full-precision model together with downstream accuracy. Our benchmark suites cover standard language understanding (MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, TruthfulQA, and IFEval); mathematical and scientific reasoning (MATH-500, GSM8K, AIME, HMMT, MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, and GPQA-Diamond); coding (LiveCodeBench and OJBench); and long-context performance (LongBench-v2, RULER, MRCR, and LongProc).

## 6 Conclusion
We introduce QUASAR, which integrates loss-aware reconstruction into the training loop to lower the loss floor of QAT. QUASAR combines scale search with a saliency-weighted dequantization fit, using an exponential moving average of squared gradients as the saliency signal. It adds only 1.4% latency overhead per training step and incurs no inference overhead. Our theoretical analysis shows that loss-aware reconstruction error directly controls QAT convergence and the quantized model's final loss, providing a principled foundation for QUASAR's objective. We evaluate QUASAR across four model families and four quantization formats. Across these settings, QUASAR achieves lower training and evaluation loss than competitive QAT baselines and improves downstream accuracy over QAT and PTQ methods. Notably, QUASAR's INT4 Gemma-4 checkpoint outperforms Google's QAT checkpoint in KL divergence and benchmark accuracy.

### A.1 Algorithm Statements for Deployment Formats
QUASAR applies to affine and symmetric integer formats, NVFP4, and MXFP4. Each variant follows Algorithm 1: it searches over code assignments, fits the allowed dequantization parameters when the format permits, and selects the reconstruction with the lowest loss-aware error. The code grid and stored parameters depend on the format. Table 5 summarizes the variants.
QUASAR checkpoints use standard layouts supported by vLLM's quantization backends. In vLLM, Marlin and Machete serve INT4 weights, NVFP4 runs natively on Blackwell GPUs, and MXFP4 weight-only checkpoints are supported. INT3 and INT2 currently lack dedicated fast kernels, so we treat them as accuracy frontiers rather than deployment formats.

Algorithm 2: QUASAR for affine integer quantization. Candidate ranges assign codes; weighted least squares then fits the stored scale and offset.
All candidates are evaluated in one batched kernel. The assignment parameters are temporary; the best codes and fitted scale and offset define the reconstruction. Backpropagation uses the same STE as standard QAT. This adds 1.4% to the step time (Table 4) and preserves the standard RTN/GPTQ inference layout.
Symmetric integer formats. Symmetric integer formats store one scale per group and no zero-point, so r_i=sq_i. For fixed signed codes, weighted least squares gives s⋆ = (Σ_i h_iq_iw_i)/(Σ_i h_iq_i²). This is the GPTQ layout used by W4A16 and W4A8 serving kernels.
Algorithm 3: QUASAR for symmetric integer quantization. Signed codes use a zero-centered assignment, and weighted least squares fits one scale.
Affine integer formats with an integer zero-point. Some affine layouts, including AWQ, store an integer zero-point q_0 and reconstruct weights as r_i=s(q_i−q_0). For each code assignment, QUASAR first computes the continuous fit, rounds q_0=−z⋆/s⋆ to a valid code, and refits the scale: q_0 = clamp(round(−z⋆/s⋆), 0, 2^b−1), s⋆_{q_0} = (Σ_i h_i(q_i−q_0) w_i)/(Σ_i h_i(q_i−q_0)²). Algorithm 4 applies this constraint to every candidate.
Algorithm 4: QUASAR for affine integer quantization with an integer zero-point. The continuous fit is snapped to an integer zero-point, then the scale is refit.
NVFP4. NVFP4 represents groups of 16 weights with E2M1 codes, an E4M3 scale per group, and a tensor-level FP32 scale σ. It is symmetric and has no zero-point. We set σ from the tensor's maximum absolute weight and keep it fixed. For each candidate, QUASAR rounds the assignment scale to E4M3, assigns E2M1 codes, and fits the stored E4M3 dequantization scale. It keeps the reconstruction with the lowest loss-aware error (Algorithm 5).
Algorithm 5: QUASAR for NVFP4. E2M1 codes use E4M3-rounded assignment and fitted group scales under a fixed FP32 tensor scale.
MXFP4. MXFP4 stores E2M1 codes in groups of 32 with one E8M0 power-of-two scale per group. Because the stored scale is discrete, QUASAR searches the E8M0 scale grid directly rather than fitting a continuous scale. Saliency h determines which candidate has the lowest loss-aware error (Algorithm 6).
Algorithm 6: QUASAR for MXFP4. E2M1 codes share an E8M0 power-of-two scale, so scale search also determines the stored dequantization scale.

### A.2 Choice of saliency weighting
QUASAR weights squared reconstruction error by saliency h_t, our diagonal curvature estimate. A natural alternative is h_t², motivated by the squared linearized gradient mismatch: ‖H_t(r_t−w_t)‖² =(r_t−w_t)ᵀH_t²(r_t−w_t). We compare both choices with all other settings fixed: AdamW at INT2, INT3, and INT4, and SGD at three stable INT2 learning rates. Weighting by h_t gives lower held-out loss in all six settings. Weighting by h_t² is unstable at INT4 and at the higher SGD learning rates (Figure 9).

## B Proofs and Deferred Theory Discussion
This appendix contains the proofs of the results stated in Section 4.

### B.1 Proofs of the main results
We use the notation 𝔼_t[·] introduced in Section 4 and write H(y):=∇²ℒ(y) for the Hessian at y. We first record a sufficient condition for Assumption 3. Fix any reconstruction index t under consideration, including the terminal index t=T, and put d_t=r_t−w_t, D_t=diag(h_t). Suppose that, at every point y=w_t+u d_t on the reconstruction segment, H(y)² ⪯ C D_t, 0 ≤ u ≤ 1. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, Jensen's inequality, and the symmetry of the Hessian, ‖∇ℒ(r_t)−∇ℒ(w_t)‖² = ‖∫_0¹ H(w_t+u d_t)d_t du‖² ≤ ∫_0¹ d_tᵀ H(w_t+u d_t)²d_t du ≤ C d_tᵀ D_t d_t =CŜ_t. Thus (9) implies Assumption 3. In particular, if ℒ is L-smooth and h_{t,i} ≥ h_min >0, then, for every y,d∈ℝ^d, ‖H(y)d‖² ≤ L²‖d‖² ≤ L²/h_min Σ_i h_{t,i}d_i². Consequently, Assumption 3 holds with C=L²/h_min.
Proof of Theorem 1. For part (i), set a_t=∇ℒ(w_t), c_t=∇ℒ(r_t). By smoothness and the update in Assumption 2, ℒ(w_{t+1}) ≤ ℒ(w_t) −η⟨a_t,c_t+ξ_t⟩ + Lη²/2 ‖c_t+ξ_t‖². Conditional on ℋ_t, both a_t and c_t are fixed. Moreover, 𝔼_t‖c_t+ξ_t‖² =‖c_t‖²+𝔼_t‖ξ_t‖² ≤ (1+M)‖c_t‖²+σ². It follows that 𝔼_t[ℒ(w_{t+1})] ≤ ℒ(w_t) −η⟨a_t,c_t⟩+ Lη²/2 ((1+M)‖c_t‖²+σ²). Using the identity 2⟨a_t,c_t⟩=‖a_t‖²+‖c_t‖²−‖c_t−a_t‖² and Assumption 3, we obtain 𝔼_t[ℒ(w_{t+1})] ≤ ℒ(w_t) − η/2 ‖a_t‖² − (η(1−Lη(1+M)))/2 ‖c_t‖² + ηC/2 Ŝ_t + Lη²/2 σ². We take expectations and sum this inequality for 0 ≤ t<T. The terms involving the loss telescope, and therefore η/2 Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)𝔼[‖∇ℒ(w_t)‖² +(1−Lη(1+M))‖∇ℒ(r_t)‖²] ≤ ℒ(w_0)−𝔼ℒ(w_T) + ηC/2 Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)𝔼Ŝ_t + Lη²T/2 σ². Since ℒ(w_T) ≥ ℒ⋆, the desired result follows after division by ηT/2. This proves part (i).
For part (ii), put γ=1−ημ. The assumption on η ensures that 0<γ<1 and that 1−Lη(1+M)>0. We may thus omit the term involving ‖c_t‖² in (10). The PL inequality at w_t gives 𝔼[ℒ(w_{t+1})−ℒ⋆] ≤ γ 𝔼[ℒ(w_t)−ℒ⋆] + ηC/2 𝔼Ŝ_t + Lη²/2 σ². Iteration yields 𝔼[ℒ(w_T)−ℒ⋆] ≤ γ^T(ℒ(w_0)−ℒ⋆) + ηC/2 Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)γ^(T−1−t)𝔼Ŝ_t + Lησ²/2μ (1−γ^T).
It remains to compare w_T and r_T. Applying the smoothness inequality at w_T with the point w_T−L⁻¹∇ℒ(w_T), we get ℒ(w_T− 1/L ∇ℒ(w_T)) ≤ ℒ(w_T)− 1/2L ‖∇ℒ(w_T)‖². The left-hand side is at least ℒ⋆, and hence ‖∇ℒ(w_T)‖² ≤ 2L(ℒ(w_T)−ℒ⋆). Assumption 3 at the terminal index t=T gives ‖∇ℒ(r_T)−∇ℒ(w_T)‖² ≤ CŜ_T. By the PL inequality at r_T and ‖x+y‖² ≤ 2‖x‖²+2‖y‖², it follows that ℒ(r_T)−ℒ⋆ ≤ 1/2μ ‖∇ℒ(r_T)‖² ≤ 1/μ ‖∇ℒ(w_T)‖² + 1/μ ‖∇ℒ(r_T)−∇ℒ(w_T)‖² ≤ 2L/μ (ℒ(w_T)−ℒ⋆) + C/μ Ŝ_T. Taking expectations and substituting (11) yields the exact intermediate bound 𝔼[ℒ(r_T)−ℒ⋆] ≤ 2L/μ γ^T(ℒ(w_0)−ℒ⋆) + LηC/μ Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)γ^(T−1−t)𝔼Ŝ_t + L²ησ²/μ² (1−γ^T) + C/μ 𝔼Ŝ_T. The random index τ in part (ii) is independent of the training randomness and has distribution Pr(τ=t) = (ημγ^(T−1−t))/(1−γ^T), t∈{0,…,T−1}, so later training steps receive greater weight. Because 1−γ=ημ, these probabilities sum to one, and independence gives 𝔼Ŝ_τ = Σ_{t=0}^(T−1) (ημγ^(T−1−t))/(1−γ^T) 𝔼Ŝ_t, Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)γ^(T−1−t)𝔼Ŝ_t = (1−γ^T)/ημ 𝔼Ŝ_τ. Thus the second term in (12) equals LC/μ² (1−γ^T)𝔼Ŝ_τ. Since the reconstruction errors are nonnegative and 1−γ^T ≤ 1, this is at most LC/μ² 𝔼Ŝ_τ. Applying the same bound to the noise term and recalling γ=1−ημ gives (5), proving part (ii).

### B.2 Deferred discussion of the assumptions and the bound
Remark 1 (Assumptions). Assumptions 1 and 2 are standard in stochastic optimization. Assumption 1 requires a twice continuously differentiable, smooth objective with a finite infimum, but not convexity. Assumption 2 allows gradient noise to grow with the gradient norm and recovers bounded variance when M=0; the same condition is used to analyze biased SGD.
Assumption 3 is specific to reconstruction: small loss-aware reconstruction error must imply a small gradient mismatch. Appendix B.1 derives a sufficient curvature condition. Under L-smoothness and a positive saliency floor h_{t,i} ≥ h_min >0, it holds with C=L²/h_min. Adam's second moment motivates the saliency estimate but does not guarantee the condition. Empirically, saliency tracks the Hessian diagonal, and the full quadratic form is nearly proportional to Ŝ (Figure 15, Appendix C).
Assumption 4 is the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition and is needed only for part (ii) of Theorem 1. Strong convexity implies PL, but PL does not require convexity and ensures that every stationary point is a global minimum. Local PL-type conditions have also been established for some overparameterized neural networks.
Comparison with standard and biased SGD. To compare the guarantees, define B_T:= (ℒ(w_0)−ℒ⋆)/ηT +ησ², S̅_T:= 1/T Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)𝔼Ŝ_t. Let α_η:=1−Lη(1+M)>0. Up to constant factors, the relevant bounds are Standard SGD: 1/T Σ_{t=0}^(T−1) 𝔼‖∇ℒ(w_t)‖² ≲ B_T, Biased SGD: 1/T Σ_{t=0}^(T−1) 𝔼‖∇ℒ(w_t)‖² ≲ (B_T+ζ²)/(1−m), QUASAR: 1/T Σ_{t=0}^(T−1)𝔼[‖∇ℒ(w_t)‖²+α_η‖∇ℒ(r_t)‖²] ≲ B_T+CS̅_T. Biased-SGD analysis assumes ‖b_t‖² ≤ m‖∇ℒ(w_t)‖²+ζ² with 0 ≤ m<1. In QAT, the bias is the reconstruction-induced gradient mismatch b_t=e_t. Assumption 3 bounds this mismatch by CŜ_t, which QUASAR minimizes at every step. The resulting bound contains the usual SGD terms plus CS̅_T and controls stationarity at both the latent and reconstructed weights.

## D Experimental Details
This appendix specifies the training and evaluation protocols for Section 5. Format-specific algorithms are given in Appendix A.1, and released checkpoints are listed in Appendix H.
Quantization scope. The controlled INT experiments use asymmetric weight-only INT4, INT3, or INT2 quantization with group size 128. We quantize every linear projection in the transformer blocks and keep activations, embeddings, the language-model head, and norms in BF16. Healing updates only the quantized linear weights; adaptation updates all student parameters.
Healing. We apply QAD for 4,096 steps at sequence length 4,096. Qwen3-4B uses global batch 32 and learning rates 2,3,5×10⁻⁵ at INT4, INT3, and INT2. Llama-3.1-8B uses global batch 128 and learning rates 1,2,3×10⁻⁵. This corresponds to roughly 60k supervised tokens per step. Training uses KL distillation from the full-precision teacher. QUASAR uses AdamW's second moment as saliency and searches clipping factors from 0.30 to 1.00 in increments of 0.05.
Adaptation. We train Qwen3-4B-Base on OpenMathReasoning for 4,096 steps at sequence length 18,432 and global batch 8. All methods use cross-entropy, AdamW with learning rate 5×10⁻⁵, a cosine schedule with 1% warmup, no weight decay, and gradient clipping at 1. QUASAR uses the same integer quantizer and scale-search grid as in healing.
NVFP4. NVFP4 QAD uses AdamW with learning rate 10⁻⁶ and global batch 32. We materialize the final checkpoints and evaluate them through vLLM's native NVFP4 path.
Baselines. Standard QAT uses fixed min–max ranges; LSQ learns the scales; Denoising QAT refits the dequantization parameters; BitDistiller combines clipping with self-distillation; RTN rounds to the nearest grid point; GPTQ minimizes a second-order reconstruction error; and AWQ protects activation-salient weights.
Evaluation protocol. For healing, we report fidelity to the full-precision model and downstream accuracy. For adaptation, we report held-out perplexity and accuracy on five math benchmarks. NVFP4 results include fidelity, reasoning, coding, and long-context evaluations. Full task protocols are given with the corresponding results in Appendices E, F, and G.
